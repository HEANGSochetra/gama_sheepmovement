Lower flock spacing means stronger gathering.
Evaluation
- 20 versus 60 sheep: At cycle 15, experiments 1 and 2 have almost identical flock spacing. There is no strong evidence that 60 sheep gather better in this short run. However, 60 sheep trampled approximately 2.75 times more area.
- Vegetation: Experiments 3 and 4 produced the tightest groups, with spacing close to 1.07 m. Vegetation therefore appears to be the strongest gathering factor.
- Dog: Comparing 3→5 and 4→6, the dog increased spacing by roughly 49–55%. The dog disturbed the flock and caused it to spread. With 60 sheep, it also increased movement and trampling.
- Five obstacles: Experiments 7 and 8 remained relatively cohesive, but spacing was greater than in vegetation-only experiments. Obstacles produced 156 and 369 encounters and slightly reduced the trampled area.
- Twenty obstacles: Experiment 9 had the largest spacing, 5.94 m. This indicates that many obstacles fragmented the 20-sheep flock. With 60 sheep, the flock remained much tighter because the higher density made nearby neighbours easier to find.
- Dog and ten obstacles: Experiment 11 showed reduced movement and considerable separation. Experiment 12 remained more cohesive, but generated many more encounters and approximately three times as much trampled area.
Main conclusion
Vegetation encourages sheep to gather. Dogs disturb and spread the flock. Obstacles restrict movement and reduce the area being trampled, but many obstacles can divide a small flock. Increasing the population from 20 to 60 greatly increases environmental impact, while often helping the flock remain connected under difficult conditions.
For a defensible final study, run every experiment for the same 50 or 500 cycles, repeat each case at least 10 times, report mean ± standard deviation, and divide obstacle encounters by sheep count. The current screenshots show one random run per treatment, so unusual results—especially experiments 9 and 11—may be caused partly by random placement.
